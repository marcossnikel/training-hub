# Fueling fundamentals for endurance running

## Definition

Fueling is the matching of energy, carbohydrate, protein, fluid, and micronutrient availability to health, training, recovery, and competition demands. It is not synonymous with consuming carbohydrate during every run.

The primary questions are:

- Is total energy intake sufficient across the day and week?
- Does carbohydrate availability match the purpose and intensity of the session?
- Is there enough carbohydrate and protein to recover before the next important load?
- Can the gut tolerate the race strategy under realistic pace, heat, and fluid conditions?
- Does the plan preserve health, bone, endocrine function, sleep, and relationship with food?

## Governing principle

Nutrition should support the work required. High carbohydrate availability is most valuable when session quality, long duration, rapid recovery, or competition performance matters. Lower intake during a short easy session can be normal without intentionally creating chronic low energy availability.

### Claim

Well-chosen nutrition strategies improve endurance performance and recovery, but the appropriate amount and timing depend on the session, athlete, environment, and recovery window. [SRC-020][SRC-021]

**Evidence:** Strong\
**Population:** Athletes across sports and levels\
**Type:** Consensus statement + sport-nutrition synthesis\
**Confidence:** High\
**Important caveat:** Guidelines describe useful ranges, not universal minimums or maximums for every session.

## Energy availability comes first

Energy availability concerns dietary energy remaining for physiological function after exercise cost. Chronically inadequate availability can affect bone, endocrine, metabolic, immune, reproductive, and performance outcomes in female and male athletes. RED-S is not exclusively a women's issue. [SRC-025]

Underfueling can be intentional, accidental, or caused by practical barriers such as appetite suppression, early sessions, travel, food access, or training volume that rises faster than intake.

### Safety boundary

Persistent fatigue, recurrent bone injury, menstrual disturbance, loss of libido, repeated illness, disordered eating, or unexplained performance decline requires qualified clinical assessment. This repository does not estimate individual energy availability from casual food logs or diagnose RED-S.

## Carbohydrate availability

Muscle glycogen supplies the working muscle; liver glycogen helps maintain blood glucose. Exercise intensity and duration determine how quickly carbohydrate becomes performance-limiting. High-intensity work depends especially on carbohydrate because fat oxidation cannot supply ATP at the same rate. [SRC-021]

Carbohydrate availability can be increased through intake before, during, and after exercise. Its usefulness depends on the next task:

- a short easy run after normal meals may need no special product;
- threshold or VO2 work benefits from starting with adequate availability;
- a long run creates both performance and gut-training needs;
- two demanding sessions close together increase the importance of rapid replenishment;
- a marathon plan should be rehearsed at event-specific pace and conditions.

## Before training

Pre-session intake should consider time since the last meal, session duration/intensity, gastrointestinal tolerance, and total daily energy needs. The objective is not to maximize grams mechanically; it is to arrive with enough substrate and comfort to execute the purpose.

Low-fiber, familiar carbohydrate can be useful before long or hard running. Athletes with early-morning sessions may use smaller or liquid options. Individual tolerability matters more than an idealized meal template.

## During training: duration context

### Under 60 minutes

For a normally fed athlete, exogenous carbohydrate is usually not physiologically necessary for an easy session. For a hard effort approaching one hour, small carbohydrate exposure or mouth sensing/rinsing can have central performance effects even when glycogen is not depleted. [SRC-021]

### 60–90 minutes

Need depends heavily on intensity, pre-session intake, and the next session. Carbohydrate can support hard work and recovery; an easy run may require little during-session intake if total energy intake is adequate.

### 90–150 minutes

Carbohydrate becomes increasingly useful for maintaining output, reducing perceived effort, and rehearsing tolerance. Traditional guidance often uses 30–60 g/h in this range, but the useful dose is contextual and should be progressed. [SRC-021][SRC-022]

### More than 2.5 hours

Traditional guidance extends up to about 90 g/h using multiple transportable carbohydrates. Recent research shows that selected trained athletes can oxidize and tolerate 100–120 g/h under controlled conditions, but performance superiority over 90 g/h is not yet established across runners or events. [SRC-039][SRC-040][SRC-041]

### Ultra-endurance

Duration increases the importance of total energy, food variety, fluid/sodium integration, and gastrointestinal management. A carbohydrate target cannot be separated from pacing, heat, fluid concentration, and tolerance. Trail/ultra findings should not automatically become road-marathon rules.

## After training

Recovery urgency depends on time until the next demanding session. When recovery time is long, normal meals can restore glycogen. When another high-quality session follows within hours, aggressive early carbohydrate intake is more valuable; adding protein supports muscle remodeling and can help practical recovery. [SRC-020]

The post-session window is not a minute-by-minute cliff. It is a period whose importance rises as the next performance demand gets closer.

## Gut training

The gastrointestinal system can adapt to repeated feeding during exercise. A 2023 systematic review included only eight heterogeneous studies, generally lasting 4–28 days; some showed reduced discomfort or malabsorption, while symptom and barrier-function results were inconsistent. [SRC-023]

### Claim

Practicing the intended carbohydrate and fluid strategy during representative exercise can improve tolerance for some athletes, but evidence does not support one universal gut-training protocol. [SRC-023]

**Evidence:** Moderate\
**Population:** Endurance athletes, mostly small samples\
**Type:** Systematic review of feeding-challenge interventions\
**Confidence:** Moderate\
**Important caveat:** Heat, running impact, dose, concentration, and starting diet change the response.

## Train-low and fasted training

Low glycogen can amplify some molecular signals associated with mitochondrial adaptation. In endurance-trained athletes, however, a meta-analysis found no overall performance benefit from periodized carbohydrate restriction versus normal carbohydrate availability; reduced training quality is a plausible cost. [SRC-024]

### Claim

An amplified molecular signal from low-carbohydrate training does not demonstrate improved race performance.

**Evidence:** Moderate\
**Population:** Endurance-trained athletes\
**Type:** Systematic review + meta-analysis\
**Confidence:** High\
**Important caveat:** Selected low-availability sessions may have individual uses, but chronic underfueling and low energy availability are different and hazardous contexts.

SWAP cautions strongly against fasted training, especially when it contributes to within-day energy deficits or endocrine risk. That is coach interpretation informed by health concerns; the independent scientific foundation is the RED-S/energy-availability literature and the absence of a clear performance advantage from train-low interventions. [SRC-025][SRC-037]

## Stimulus-cost model

### Primary benefits

- higher quality during long or intense sessions;
- maintained blood glucose and carbohydrate oxidation;
- lower perceived effort in some contexts;
- faster glycogen restoration when recovery is short;
- race-specific gastrointestinal practice;
- reduced risk of chronic accidental energy deficit.

### Primary costs

- gastrointestinal symptoms if dose/concentration is mismatched;
- dental exposure and product cost;
- carrying/logistics burden;
- false precision or anxiety around grams;
- displacement of diverse whole-food intake if products dominate daily nutrition.

## Practical implications

- Match carbohydrate availability to session purpose rather than ideology.
- Fuel hard and long work enough to execute it; do not use low output as proof of adaptation.
- Progress race carbohydrate intake in training.
- Integrate grams per hour with fluid concentration, heat, and sodium needs.
- Evaluate the entire day, not just intake during the run.
- Treat recurrent underfueling signs as health concerns, not discipline.

## Open questions

- Do 100–120 g/h strategies improve road-marathon performance beyond 90 g/h in broader populations?
- How should carbohydrate targets scale with body size, sex, pace, and environmental heat?
- Which gut-training protocol transfers best from cycling to running?
- Can selected train-low sessions add value without increasing low-energy-availability risk in real-world runners?
