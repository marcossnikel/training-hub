# Carbohydrates for endurance training and racing

## Definition

Carbohydrate availability is the access of muscle and the central nervous system to carbohydrate from muscle glycogen, liver glycogen/blood glucose, and ingested sources. Availability is session-specific and time-specific; it is not captured by one daily percentage of calories.

## Muscle versus liver glycogen

- **Muscle glycogen** is stored locally and used by the active fibers; it is not released directly as blood glucose.
- **Liver glycogen** supports blood glucose, especially between meals and during prolonged exercise.
- **Ingested carbohydrate** can maintain blood glucose and add exogenous oxidation, reducing reliance on finite internal stores or enabling higher output.

The relative contribution depends on intensity, duration, training status, prior diet, and carbohydrate intake during the session. [SRC-020][SRC-021]

## Why carbohydrate supports performance

Carbohydrate can support ATP production at high rates, preserve blood glucose, increase whole-body carbohydrate oxidation, and affect the central nervous system. A meta-analysis of 136 endurance studies found an overall performance benefit, with larger effects in longer exercise and time-to-exhaustion tests than time trials. [SRC-022]

### Claim

Carbohydrate ingestion during endurance exercise improves group-average performance, especially as duration increases, but effect size varies by test, training status, and context. [SRC-022]

**Evidence:** Strong\
**Population:** Broad endurance-exercise literature\
**Type:** Systematic review + meta-analysis/meta-regression\
**Confidence:** High\
**Important caveat:** A positive average effect does not show that more carbohydrate is always better or that every short easy session needs intake.

## Transport and oxidation

Glucose uses intestinal transport pathways that can become rate limiting. Fructose uses a different transporter, so combined glucose/fructose ingestion can increase total absorption and exogenous oxidation at high intake rates. Ratios in research have evolved from roughly 2:1 toward approximately 1:0.8 or 1:1 at very high intakes. [SRC-021][SRC-039][SRC-040]

Transporter theory does not guarantee tolerance. Solution concentration, fluid delivery, heat, exercise mode, and individual gastrointestinal history matter.

## Intake by duration

These are evidence-informed starting ranges, not laws.

| Duration/context | Evidence-aware interpretation |
|---|---|
| `<60 min`, easy | Usually no during-session carbohydrate is required when normally fed; total daily intake still matters. |
| `<60 min`, hard | Small amounts or mouth carbohydrate sensing may support performance through central effects. [SRC-021] |
| `60–90 min` | Intake can be useful when intensity is high, pre-session availability is low, or rapid recovery matters; needs vary widely. |
| `90–150 min` | `30–60 g/h` is a common evidence-based range; higher doses can be rehearsed for race specificity. [SRC-021] |
| `>2.5 h` | Up to about `90 g/h` from multiple transportable carbohydrates is established guidance for demanding endurance exercise. [SRC-020][SRC-021] |
| `100–120 g/h` | Promising for high oxidation and selected trained athletes; direct performance superiority and recreational-running applicability remain limited. [SRC-039][SRC-040][SRC-041] |

Body size may influence absolute energy demand, but intestinal transport does not scale cleanly with body mass. Therefore grams per hour should not be mechanically converted to grams per kilogram per hour during exercise without evidence for the context.

## 60 versus 90 versus 120 g/h

### 60 g/h

Single-source glucose oxidation tends to approach a ceiling around this range, though individual responses and protocols vary. It remains a substantial intake and may be adequate for many marathoners depending on duration, pre-race stores, pace, and tolerance.

### 90 g/h

Multiple transportable carbohydrates can raise exogenous oxidation beyond glucose alone. This range has the strongest guideline history for events longer than roughly 2.5 hours. [SRC-020][SRC-021]

### 100–120 g/h

Controlled studies have shown that trained male cyclists can tolerate and oxidize 120 g/h after high-carbohydrate preparation, with little distress in small samples. A recent study in eight elite male marathoners found higher exogenous and whole-body carbohydrate oxidation at 120 versus 60/90 g/h, but explicitly left performance implications unresolved. [SRC-040][SRC-041]

### Claim

Evidence that athletes can oxidize or tolerate 120 g/h does not yet establish 120 g/h as superior race fueling for marathon runners generally.

**Evidence:** Limited\
**Population:** Small samples of trained men and elite male marathoners\
**Type:** Randomized crossover physiology studies\
**Confidence:** High about the limitation\
**Important caveat:** Newer durability and performance trials may change the boundary; review the source index date before use.

## Running versus cycling

Many high-dose studies use cycling, which reduces impact and allows easier feeding. Running can increase gastrointestinal jostling and reduce tolerance. A dose successful on the bike should be validated at target running pace and environmental conditions before racing.

## Gut training protocol principles

Evidence supports repeated exposure, but not one canonical protocol. [SRC-023]

1. Start with a currently tolerable dose.
2. Use the intended carbohydrate types and delivery forms.
3. Practice at progressively more representative duration and intensity.
4. Integrate fluid so concentration is realistic.
5. Change one major variable at a time.
6. Record upper/lower GI symptoms, perceived fullness, intake actually consumed, pace, and heat.
7. Regress after symptoms rather than forcing the target.

## Pre-exercise carbohydrate

The useful amount depends on time since the prior meal, session demand, and tolerance. The goal is adequate liver and muscle carbohydrate availability, not compulsory eating immediately before every run. An athlete with a normal meal several hours earlier differs from an athlete training after an overnight fast.

## Post-exercise replenishment

When rapid recovery is needed, early high-rate carbohydrate feeding supports glycogen restoration. When the next hard session is not close, total intake across normal meals is more important than minute-level timing. [SRC-020]

## Train low versus perform high

Low glycogen can amplify AMPK/PGC-1α-associated signaling, but chronic performance evidence is not clearly improved in trained athletes. A systematic review/meta-analysis found no overall performance advantage from periodized carbohydrate restriction and identified reduced interval quality as a key tradeoff. [SRC-024]

### Claim

“Biochemical adaptation” and “race-performance improvement” are different outcomes.

**Evidence:** Moderate\
**Population:** Endurance-trained athletes\
**Type:** Mechanistic evidence + intervention meta-analysis\
**Confidence:** High\
**Important caveat:** The absence of a mean performance advantage does not prove every individualized train-low use is ineffective; health and energy availability remain constraints.

## Environmental effects

Heat can change fluid delivery, gut blood flow, perceived sweetness, gastric comfort, and endogenous carbohydrate use. A grams-per-hour plan must be tested with the intended fluid volume and ambient conditions. More concentrated intake without adequate fluid can worsen symptoms for some athletes.

## Primary benefits

- sustain higher-intensity ATP demand;
- maintain blood glucose;
- support total and exogenous carbohydrate oxidation;
- reduce perceived effort in some settings;
- maintain session quality and later-day recovery;
- rehearse competition intake.

## Primary costs and constraints

- GI distress and malabsorption;
- fluid/carbohydrate concentration conflicts;
- product logistics and dental exposure;
- satiety that displaces total energy intake;
- mistaken escalation from a guideline to an obligation.

## Best fit for high intake

- Long, demanding races where carbohydrate oxidation is performance-limiting.
- Trained athletes who have progressed tolerance under representative conditions.
- Athletes who can ingest the dose without compromising fluid, pace, or comfort.

## Poor fit for high intake

- First use on race day.
- Short easy sessions with no recovery urgency.
- Athletes with unresolved GI symptoms who increase dose and concentration together.
- Copying an elite male cycling protocol into recreational running without testing.

## Open questions

- What is the performance dose-response above 90 g/h in road marathon running?
- Do women and smaller athletes show similar absorption, oxidation, and tolerance at very high absolute doses?
- How much of a high-dose benefit comes from oxidation versus central or durability effects?
- Which glucose/fructose ratio is best at different absolute intakes and fluid volumes?
