# Running economy

## Definition

Running economy is the oxygen or energy cost of running at a specified submaximal speed under specified conditions. Lower cost at the same speed is usually better, but the measurement depends on speed, footwear, grade, environment, metabolic steady state, and calculation method.

Economy is not identical to form, stiffness, efficiency, VO2max, or performance. It is an integrated outcome of metabolic, biomechanical, neuromuscular, anthropometric, and equipment effects. [SRC-001][SRC-014]

## Why it matters

At similar VO2max and fractional utilization, the runner who uses less energy at a target speed has more physiological reserve. Economy also changes with speed and fatigue, so one fresh-state value cannot fully represent marathon performance.

### Claim

High-load strength training and some combined strength methods can produce small-to-moderate improvements in running economy when added to endurance training. [SRC-013]

**Evidence:** Moderate\
**Population:** Moderately to highly trained middle- and long-distance runners\
**Type:** Systematic review + meta-analysis of controlled studies\
**Confidence:** Moderate\
**Important caveat:** Improved economy does not establish injury prevention, and group-average effects do not identify the best exercise for an individual runner.

## Physiological and neuromuscular determinants

- muscle oxidative capacity and fiber recruitment;
- motor coordination and co-contraction;
- elastic energy storage and return;
- muscle-tendon stiffness within a useful task-specific range;
- force production relative to contact time;
- respiratory and thermoregulatory work;
- footwear mass, geometry, foam, and bending stiffness;
- fatigue-related changes in all of the above.

These determinants interact. Increasing one isolated variable, such as stiffness or cadence, does not guarantee better whole-body economy.

## Biomechanics: association is not a form prescription

A 2024 systematic review of 51 observational studies found that isolated biomechanical variables explained only a modest share of between-runner economy differences. Higher cadence had a small association with lower cost; smaller vertical displacement and higher vertical/leg stiffness had moderate associations. Many popular variables, including contact time, stride length, joint angles, and peak vertical force, were not consistently associated. [SRC-014]

### Claim

Observed biomechanical correlates of economical runners do not show that consciously forcing those mechanics will improve economy.

**Evidence:** Moderate for the association; limited for causal retraining\
**Population:** Healthy adult runners across heterogeneous training levels\
**Type:** Observational meta-analysis\
**Confidence:** High about the causal limitation\
**Important caveat:** Economy can change without a visible form change, and a form change can redistribute tissue load without reducing metabolic cost.

## Strength and plyometrics

High-load resistance training, plyometrics, and combined approaches may improve economy through force capacity, recruitment, stiffness, or reduced relative force demand at each stride. The 2024 meta-analysis found moderate-certainty evidence for small effects from high-load work and benefits from some combined approaches, with variation by testing speed and athlete VO2max. Submaximal and isometric methods did not show consistent benefits in the included evidence. [SRC-013]

Runner-performance syntheses also show why method labels matter. High-load work has the clearest pooled signal for economy and time-trial performance; plyometrics produce useful neuromuscular adaptations but less consistent economy and performance effects. Combined programs may perform well on average, but they also add methods and recovery cost. [SRC-045][SRC-046][SRC-047]

This supports strength as a performance tool. It does not justify the claim that a generic strength program prevents running injuries. A large runner-specific trial and a 2024 prevention meta-analysis found no overall preventive effect; those results also do not prove that every supervised or individualized intervention is ineffective. [SRC-042][SRC-049]

For the full prescription and tissue boundaries, see `../strength/strength-training-for-runners.md`, `../strength/plyometrics-and-reactive-strength.md`, and `../strength/tendon-bone-and-tissue-adaptation.md`.

## Strides, hills, and speed work

Short, fast, relaxed running is commonly used to maintain exposure to higher force and speed with limited metabolic volume. The mechanism is plausible, and the practice is widespread, but direct long-term trials showing that strides alone improve economy are sparse.

### Coaching hypothesis

Short strides or hill strides may maintain speed skill and neuromuscular recruitment while adding less metabolic fatigue than a full interval session. [SRC-036]

**Evidence:** Limited/direct; moderate/mechanistic\
**Coach practice:** Common in SWAP and many road-running systems\
**Potential benefit:** Speed familiarity, coordination, economy maintenance\
**Potential cost:** High local force and tendon/muscle stress if introduced aggressively\
**Best fit:** Healthy runners with enough tissue tolerance to run fast without straining

## Footwear

Footwear can acutely change measured economy. Modern advanced footwear usually combines several features, so attributing an effect to a carbon plate alone is difficult. An acute laboratory improvement is meaningful for racing but is not the same as a chronic physiological adaptation. [SRC-014]

Body mass and shoe mass affect oxygen cost, but “lighter is faster” is not a safe body-composition rule. Weight loss can reduce health, recovery, bone strength, and sustainable power when it creates low energy availability. [SRC-025]

## Economy under fatigue

During prolonged running, oxygen cost at the same speed can rise because of substrate shift, altered recruitment, muscle damage, thermoregulation, and biomechanical change. Trained runners appear to resist deterioration better than active but untrained adults at the same relative intensity. [SRC-011]

Fresh economy and economy durability are distinct measurements. A runner can be economical when fresh and deteriorate quickly, or be less economical initially but more stable.

A ten-week maximal-strength-plus-plyometrics intervention improved economy durability after 90 minutes in a small trial of well-trained men. [SRC-061] It is promising direct evidence, but one combined intervention cannot identify whether heavy lifting, jumps, their interaction, or another program feature produced the result.

## What we know with confidence

- Economy is multifactorial and speed-specific.
- High-load or combined strength work can improve group-average economy in trained runners. [SRC-013]
- Isolated biomechanical correlates explain limited variance and are not automatic retraining targets. [SRC-014]
- Prolonged running can worsen economy, with important between-athlete variation. [SRC-009][SRC-011]
- Acute footwear effects should not be mislabeled as training adaptation.

## What remains uncertain

- Which strength exercises and doses best fit different running phenotypes.
- Whether strides or hill strides independently improve long-term economy.
- Which mechanics are modifiable without increasing injury exposure elsewhere.
- How much fresh economy versus economy durability should guide marathon training.
- Whether a gain measured at one laboratory speed transfers to race pace and fatigued conditions.

## Primary stimulus

Economy-directed training can target force capacity, elastic behavior, speed coordination, oxidative efficiency, or resistance to late-race deterioration.

## Primary costs

- heavy strength: neuromuscular soreness and scheduling interference;
- plyometrics/sprints: high tendon and bone loading;
- high-volume economy exposure: cumulative impact and time;
- gait retraining: attentional cost and possible load redistribution;
- advanced footwear: cost, altered load distribution, and variable individual response.

## Best fit

- Runners whose aerobic profile is stronger than their economy.
- Trained runners who can add strength without displacing essential running or recovery.
- Marathoners who need late-session economy as well as fresh-state economy.

## Poor fit

- Introducing maximal lifting, plyometrics, and sprinting simultaneously.
- Forcing a generic “ideal” form from observational correlates.
- Chasing lower body mass while energy availability or bone health is compromised.
- Adding economy work when pain or accumulated fatigue already changes mechanics.

## Open questions

- What is the minimum effective strength dose after an economy gain has been achieved?
- Are economy responders to advanced footwear also responders to specific strength methods?
- Which field measures best capture economy deterioration without laboratory gas analysis?
