# Physiological durability and fatigue resistance

## Definition

Durability is the time of onset and magnitude of deterioration in physiological or performance characteristics during or after prolonged exercise. It asks how much of an athlete's fresh-state VO2max, threshold, economy, or power-duration profile remains after substantial work. [SRC-009]

“Fatigue resistance” is often used as a practical synonym. “Physiological resilience” is another research term. This repository uses `durability` as the canonical topic and names the specific variable that deteriorates.

## Durability is not simply endurance

Endurance describes the ability to sustain exercise. Durability describes the stability of underlying characteristics while exercise accumulates. An athlete may finish a long event through lower initial output, fueling, and pacing while still showing considerable physiological deterioration. Another may have high fresh capacity but lose it rapidly.

Durability is therefore not one latent score until measurement protocols become more standardized. Economy durability, threshold durability, critical-speed durability, neuromuscular durability, and cardiac/heart-rate drift can disagree.

## Measurement

A useful durability assessment has:

1. a fresh-state measure;
2. a controlled fatiguing task;
3. the same measure during or after the task;
4. the absolute and relative change;
5. controlled fueling, hydration, temperature, and pacing.

The fatiguing task's intensity and duration materially change the result. Comparing athletes or tests without matching relative work, carbohydrate intake, environment, and measurement timing can be misleading. [SRC-009]

### Common field signals

- pace slowing at similar grade and effort;
- heart-rate drift at constant pace or power;
- pace-heart-rate decoupling;
- rising oxygen cost or RPE at the same output;
- deterioration in critical speed, threshold marker, or finishing power;
- altered stride mechanics or ground contact;
- reduced force or jump output.

These signals are not interchangeable. Heart-rate drift can reflect heat, dehydration, plasma-volume change, and substrate use; it is not a direct measure of mitochondrial failure.

## Candidate mechanisms

- muscle and liver glycogen depletion;
- increasing reliance on fat oxidation at a given output;
- recruitment of less-efficient fibers as earlier fibers fatigue;
- muscle damage and excitation-contraction impairment;
- altered tendon and muscle behavior;
- changing biomechanics and co-contraction;
- reduced plasma volume and thermoregulatory strain;
- central and peripheral neuromuscular fatigue;
- reduced attainable VO2max or downward shift of metabolic boundaries;
- pacing, motivation, discomfort, and environmental stress.

These can co-occur. A mechanism plausible in a laboratory protocol should not be assumed to dominate every marathon.

### Claim

Trained runners resist deterioration in running economy and attainable VO2max better than active untrained adults during one hour of running at the same relative intensity. [SRC-011]

**Evidence:** Limited\
**Population:** 10 trained runners and 10 active adults\
**Type:** Controlled physiological study\
**Confidence:** Moderate for this protocol\
**Important caveat:** This small comparison does not identify which training caused the difference.

### Claim

Regular long runs and higher training volume are associated with better running-economy durability in performance-matched trained male runners. [SRC-012]

**Evidence:** Observational\
**Population:** Well-trained male runners\
**Type:** Cross-sectional comparison\
**Confidence:** Moderate for association, low for causality\
**Important caveat:** Athletes who tolerate long runs or high volume may differ before training; an intervention trial is still needed.

## Training hypotheses

### Long-term volume and consistency

Years of consistent aerobic work may improve fiber oxidative capacity, capillarization, substrate handling, and tissue tolerance. Reviews consider this plausible, but direct long-term causal evidence is difficult to obtain. [SRC-010]

### Long runs

Long runs expose the athlete to prolonged substrate use, mechanical repetition, and late-session recruitment. They are specific to marathon durability, but “longer” is not monotonically better. Once mechanics, fueling, or recovery deteriorate too far, the session may accumulate cost faster than useful specific stimulus.

### Late-session intensity

Race-pace or short faster work late in a long session can test and possibly train output under fatigue. Direct evidence for the optimal structure is sparse, and the mechanical cost can be high. The best fit is a trained, well-fueled runner with stable long-run tolerance—not a developing runner using fatigue to manufacture specificity.

### Strength training

Heavy or plyometric strength can improve fresh economy and may delay recruitment of less-efficient fibers. Evidence for durability specifically is emerging and less secure than evidence for fresh economy. [SRC-010][SRC-013]

Fresh strength, jump, and economy gains should not be relabeled as fatigue resistance. A durability claim requires the relevant characteristic to be measured again after controlled prolonged work.

A 2025 randomized trial provides direct but narrow evidence: 28 well-trained male runners who added maximal strength and plyometrics for ten weeks better preserved economy after a 90-minute heavy-domain run and improved a subsequent high-intensity time-to-exhaustion test. [SRC-061] The combined intervention, male-only sample, and laboratory endpoint prevent isolating a method or predicting marathon performance. See `../strength/strength-training-for-runners.md` for fresh-state evidence and placement costs.

### Fueling

Carbohydrate during prolonged exercise supports blood glucose and carbohydrate oxidation and can preserve performance. This can improve the measured durability of a session without proving a chronic durability adaptation. Training the ability to execute well while fueled may still be practically important. [SRC-020][SRC-022]

### Coaching hypothesis: SWAP fatigue-resistance work

SWAP proposes fueled long-duration work, occasional short hard efforts after accumulated work, strength, cross-training with sustained muscular demand, heat exposure, and stress consolidation. The Roches explicitly describe several neural explanations and the “fatigue resistance snack” as hypotheses with confounding and uncertain direct evidence. [SRC-034][SRC-035]

**Evidence:** Limited to insufficient for the specific package\
**Coach practice:** SWAP, primarily trail/ultra informed\
**Potential benefit:** Specific late-session output and muscular recruitment\
**Potential cost:** Hidden accumulation of medium/hard work and local fatigue\
**Road applicability:** The general construct applies; downhill, ultra, and steep-treadmill details are not automatically road-marathon specific.

## Where the evidence conflicts or is incomplete

The field agrees that physiological characteristics deteriorate and athletes differ. It does not yet agree on one standard protocol, the independence of different durability measures, or the optimal training dose. A runner can show better economy durability because of training, pacing, fueling, heat tolerance, selection, or several at once.

Durability studies often use cycling because power is easy to control. Cycling findings can support central and metabolic mechanisms, but running adds impact, eccentric loading, and different recruitment.

## Primary stimulus

- prolonged sport-specific oxidative work;
- substrate turnover with maintained fueling;
- repeated mechanical output under controlled fatigue;
- late-session recruitment without catastrophic form deterioration.

## Primary costs

- glycogen depletion;
- muscle damage and impact exposure;
- altered mechanics;
- long recovery time;
- opportunity cost to speed and threshold work;
- increased health risk if long sessions are chronically underfueled.

## Best fit

- Marathon or ultra runners with stable baseline volume and long-run history.
- Athletes whose fresh-state physiology is stronger than late-race execution.
- Carefully controlled testing blocks where fueling and environment can be standardized.

## Poor fit

- Novices who need general consistency more than late-session complexity.
- Athletes with pain, low energy availability, or deteriorating recovery.
- Using cardiac drift alone to diagnose one physiological limitation.
- Adding hard finishes to every long run.

## Open questions

- Is durability independently trainable beyond accumulated endurance volume?
- Which durability variable best predicts road-marathon performance?
- Does late-session intensity cause more adaptation than an equivalent fresh session, or mostly more fatigue?
- How do carbohydrate dose and gut training affect durability measurement and long-term development?
- Can lower-impact cycling build central/metabolic durability that transfers meaningfully to running?
