# Cycling guide for runners

## Purpose

Cycling can add or preserve central aerobic and metabolic work with far less running impact. It does not reproduce running-specific economy, tendon/bone exposure, stiffness, or movement skill.

The correct question is not “How many running kilometers equal this ride?” It is:

> Which adaptation does the runner need, which parts can cycling supply, and what local cycling fatigue will it add?

## What transfers

### Central/general adaptations

- cardiac output and plasma-volume stimulus;
- VO2max development or maintenance;
- metabolic work and substrate turnover;
- prolonged low-impact endurance;
- high-intensity aerobic stimulus.

### Partially transferable peripheral adaptations

- mitochondrial and capillary change in the recruited leg muscles;
- muscular endurance;
- tolerance for sustained carbohydrate oxidation and fueling;
- some neuromuscular force capacity.

### Weakly transferable or non-transferable adaptations

- running economy and elastic behavior;
- impact, bone, and tendon exposure;
- downhill eccentric tolerance;
- run-specific recruitment and coordination;
- race-pace mechanics.

### Claim

Short-to-moderate cycling interventions can maintain or develop aerobic fitness, but current trials do not establish that cycling and running are interchangeable for running performance. [SRC-026][SRC-027]

**Evidence:** Limited\
**Population:** Trained and recreationally active participants in only seven modern meta-analytic trials, plus older transfer research\
**Type:** Systematic review + randomized trials + comparative physiology\
**Confidence:** Moderate\
**Important caveat:** Null between-group differences with wide confidence intervals do not prove equivalence.

## Heart rate, power, and perceived effort

Heart-rate responses differ by modality and by the athlete's history. Runners commonly reach lower cycling heart rates when local leg fatigue limits power before the cardiovascular system is maximally stressed. Experienced triathletes may show much closer modality values. [SRC-027]

Therefore:

- do not subtract a universal 10–15 bpm;
- do not assume running zones transfer unchanged;
- establish bike-specific effort from repeatable rides, talk test/RPE, and ideally bike testing;
- interpret low heart rate plus high leg fatigue as local cycling limitation, not automatically as inadequate aerobic work.

Power is valuable because it measures external output, but FTP and power zones inherit test, model, freshness, and training-history error. A runner new to cycling can have low cycling power despite high central fitness.

## Cadence

Cadence changes local force per pedal stroke, neuromuscular demand, and oxygen cost. Higher cadence often lowers torque at a given power but can raise metabolic cost for inexperienced cyclists. There is no universal optimal cadence for running transfer. Cadence should be a manipulable variable, not a compliance target.

## Z2 cycling

“Z2” must name its anchor: power, heart rate, lactate, ventilatory threshold, or RPE. For runners, a sustainable aerobic ride may still produce substantial quadriceps fatigue initially. Progress duration and power separately enough to identify which created the fatigue.

## Doubles and placement

Cycling may be attractive:

- when added running would exceed impact tolerance;
- during short-term return from a running injury when cycling is medically permitted;
- as a low-impact double after the primary running stimulus;
- as prolonged aerobic work for a runner whose schedule and recovery support it;
- to practice high-rate fueling without running jostling.

Potentially poor placements:

- before a key run when the athlete is new to cycling;
- when quadriceps fatigue changes running mechanics;
- as “recovery” that quietly becomes a hard ride;
- when additional total energy demand worsens low energy availability.

## SWAP “Improving Bike Power as a Runner” triangulation

David and Megan Roche argue that runners should become better cyclists rather than use the bike only for passive low-level work. Their 2026 Patreon article proposes 11 principles plus a fatigue-resistance bonus. These are coach practice and hypotheses, evaluated below against independent evidence. [SRC-033]

| SWAP claim | Proposed mechanism | Independent evidence | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cadence above ~85 rpm transfers better because tension is lower | Lower torque per stroke; more fatigue-resistant recruitment | Cadence changes metabolic and muscular demand, but no trial establishes >85 rpm as optimal for running transfer. [SRC-027] | **Direct evidence insufficient** |
| Use running HR zones until highly developed on the bike | Avoid underloading when local cycling power is low | Modality HR and VO2 relationships vary with training history; running and cycling zones are not reliably identical. [SRC-027] | **Partly contradicted as a universal rule** |
| Do not formally set FTP or use power zones early | Early FTP may be unstable and local power-limited | Plausible measurement caution; no direct trial shows power-based training is inferior for runners. | **Untested coaching heuristic** |
| Most rides should work into Z2 HR | Increase central stimulus per time-limited ride | General aerobic rationale is sound; using running HR zones is not established. [SRC-026][SRC-027] | **Mechanism supported; prescription untested** |
| Add short “spin-ups” | Raise neuromuscular power ceiling | Sprint/interval cycling can raise power, but direct transfer to running economy is untested. | **Mechanistic hypothesis** |
| Alternate seated and standing | Vary recruitment and sustain power | Recruitment differs; no evidence that this improves running outcomes. | **Untested** |
| Start with short hard intervals before long threshold intervals | Match the new cyclist's local power limitation | Short intervals are a plausible way to accumulate high aerobic work; runner-specific sequencing trials are absent. [SRC-002] | **Plausible, indirect** |
| Cycling permits more efforts because impact is lower | Lower musculoskeletal impact | Cycling reduces running impact, but metabolic/local fatigue and recovery still constrain quality. | **Moderately supported boundary** |
| Bike the day after a quality run | Add stimulus while avoiding recovery-shuffle impact | No direct timing trial; benefit depends on whether the ride impairs later key running. | **Untested placement heuristic** |
| One focused ride plus one secondary ride per week can progress runners | Sufficient skill and aerobic frequency | No direct dose-response trial validates two rides/week or 90–120 minutes. [SRC-026] | **Untested dose** |
| Step-ups and squats directly improve watts | Greater leg force capacity | Strength can improve running economy under some methods, but that does not validate these exercises or a 3–5× weekly step-up dose for cycling watts. [SRC-013] | **Indirect mechanism only; exact prescription untested** |
| Short efforts late in long rides train fatigue resistance | Recruit high output after prolonged work | Durability is measurable; intervention evidence for this format is sparse. [SRC-009] | **Hypothesis** |

The most defensible core of the SWAP approach is: develop enough cycling skill and local muscular capacity that the bike can deliver the intended aerobic stimulus, while monitoring whether bike fatigue damages running specificity. The least defensible elements are universal HR transfer, cadence thresholds, and exact weekly prescriptions.

## Example session tools

These are structures, not prescriptions.

### Easy aerobic ride

**Purpose:** low-impact aerobic volume.\
**Primary cost:** time, energy, saddle comfort, and local fatigue.\
**Progression:** duration before intensity if local fatigue is the limiter.

### Short aerobic-power intervals

Examples: `4–6 × 3 min hard / 3 min easy` or `2 × 6 × 30/30`.

**Purpose:** high central demand with no running impact.\
**Primary cost:** quadriceps fatigue and glycogen.\
**Good placement:** after adequate cycling familiarization, with an easy running day after.\
**Poor placement:** immediately before a key run or when power collapses from local fatigue.

### Long endurance ride

**Purpose:** prolonged aerobic/substrate work and fueling practice.\
**Primary cost:** total energy demand, saddle/position stress, and long recovery when intensity drifts upward.\
**Specificity boundary:** it does not replace the mechanical adaptation of a long run.

## Monitoring

Track separately:

- power or repeatable route output;
- bike-specific HR and RPE;
- cadence only as context;
- local leg fatigue the next morning;
- effect on key running pace/economy;
- total energy and carbohydrate demand;
- pain and offloading goal.

The best cycling dose is the one that adds the intended adaptation without reducing more valuable running consistency.

## Open questions

- What dose of cycling maximizes aerobic transfer without reducing running-specific adaptation?
- Do short bike-power intervals improve running economy independently of central fitness?
- Which modality-specific marker best regulates bike intensity for runners new to cycling?
- Can cycling durability transfer to marathon running after accounting for fueling and VO2max?
