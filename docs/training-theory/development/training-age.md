# Running training age

## Definition

Running training age is the history of consistent, running-specific exposure that an athlete has successfully absorbed. It is multidimensional and cannot be inferred from current pace, VO2max, chronological age, or one recent training block.

## Dimensions

Record at least:

- years and months of consistent running;
- typical and peak historical weekly volume;
- frequency and double-run history;
- continuity and long breaks;
- long-run history;
- exposure to threshold, severe-domain, sprint, hill, and race-specific work;
- surface and elevation history;
- strength and plyometric history;
- injury history and current symptoms;
- recovery capacity under real life stress;
- fueling and energy-availability history;
- event and race experience.

These dimensions can develop at different rates. A runner can be metabolically trained and mechanically inexperienced, or highly durable at easy mileage but inexperienced with fast running.

### Claim

Initial fitness and prior training status strongly moderate the percentage response to endurance training; less-trained participants generally show larger relative improvements. [SRC-002]

**Evidence:** Strong for group-level physiology\
**Population:** Broad human training literature, weighted toward untrained and moderately trained participants\
**Type:** Systematic review + meta-regression\
**Confidence:** High\
**Important caveat:** Baseline VO2max is only one component of running training age.

## Central fitness versus running-specific capacity

Cycling, rowing, skiing, and field sports can create substantial central aerobic fitness. Transfer to running is meaningful but incomplete because economy, impact tolerance, bone loading, tendon exposure, and coordination are sport-specific. [SRC-026][SRC-027]

### Claim

An aerobically strong cyclist who begins running may be centrally `trained` but running-specifically `novice`.

**Evidence:** Moderate/mechanistic\
**Population:** Cross-training and triathlon literature\
**Type:** Comparative + transfer evidence\
**Confidence:** High\
**Important caveat:** This classification is a reasoning aid, not a validated clinical scale.

The practical risk is that breathing and heart rate make a pace feel easy before bones, tendons, and muscles have accumulated enough running exposure. Cardiovascular capacity should not set the speed of mechanical progression by itself.

## Working categories

### Novice

- little consistent recent running;
- low weekly frequency and long-run history;
- limited exposure to faster work;
- uncertain individual response and recovery pattern.

Primary objective: establish repeatable frequency, pain-free exposure, fueling habits, and basic intensity separation.

### Developing

- consistent running is established but historical load is still modest;
- the athlete is learning which combinations of volume, intensity, and life stress are absorbable;
- tissue capacity may lag quickly improving aerobic fitness.

Primary objective: expand capacity gradually while changing few variables at once.

### Trained

- several sustained cycles at a stable workload;
- established long-run and workout history;
- known recovery responses and some resilience to normal disruption.

Primary objective: target limiting factors while preserving the consistency that built the current base.

### Highly-trained

- extensive high-volume and/or high-quality history;
- durable tolerance to sport-specific loading;
- smaller percentage gains and higher importance of detail, periodization, and fatigue management.

Primary objective: obtain small specific gains without destabilizing high-value baseline work.

### Elite

Elite adds competitive context, selection, and often professional support. It is not merely a physiology category. Elite workloads show what selected, highly adapted athletes can tolerate; they do not define safe progression for others. [SRC-004]

## Training age and volume

Historical tolerance matters more than a population mileage target. A runner stable at 80 km/week for two years and a runner who reached 80 km/week last month do not have equivalent context.

Recent change should be interpreted in multiple dimensions:

- total distance or duration;
- frequency;
- long-run duration;
- intensity time;
- speed and surface;
- vertical gain/loss;
- strength or plyometric load;
- non-running endurance load;
- fueling and recovery.

The injury literature does not validate one safe progression percentage. Associations between running parameters and injury are conflicting, and individual history modifies risk. [SRC-015][SRC-016]

## Training age and workout selection

The same workout name can represent different stress:

- 6 × 1 km near LT2 may be controlled for a runner with years of threshold work and a severe novelty for a developing runner.
- A 30 km long run may be routine exposure for one marathoner and an outsized share of weekly volume for another.
- Strides may be low metabolic cost but high mechanical novelty.
- A bike interval session may be centrally familiar but locally exhausting for a runner new to cycling.

Use training history to estimate *novelty cost* in addition to metabolic intensity.

Strength and plyometric training ages should be tracked separately. A highly trained runner can still be a novice lifter, and years of bodyweight circuits do not establish near-maximal lifting skill. See `../strength/strength-training-fundamentals.md` and `../strength/plyometrics-and-reactive-strength.md`.

## Progression model

1. Identify the adaptation sought.
2. Identify the athlete's history with the smallest effective tool.
3. Change one important stress dimension at a time where practical.
4. Hold enough of the week stable to interpret response.
5. Evaluate symptoms, performance, mood, sleep, and session quality across several exposures.
6. Progress only after the current dose is repeatable, not merely survivable.

“Repeatable” means the athlete can complete the session and preserve the following days and weeks. Finishing once does not establish capacity.

## Common mistakes

- Classifying training age by race pace.
- Treating years since the first run as years of consistent training.
- Copying an elite workload without the athlete's build-up history.
- Increasing frequency, volume, long-run share, and intensity together.
- Assuming cross-training fitness eliminates running-specific progression needs.
- Using absence of immediate pain as proof that a load increase was absorbed.

## Open questions
