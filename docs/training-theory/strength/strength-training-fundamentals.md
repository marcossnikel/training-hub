# Strength training fundamentals

## Definition

Strength training is planned exposure to external resistance with the goal of improving the capacity to produce, absorb, or transmit force. Free weights, machines, cables, bands, bodyweight, and isometrics can all qualify. The method name matters less than the force demand, movement, range, effort, volume, progression, and outcome being trained.

Strength, power, hypertrophy, local muscular endurance, tendon properties, bone loading, balance, and skill are related but not interchangeable outcomes. A program can improve one with little change in another.

## The main adaptations

### Maximal strength

Maximal strength is the greatest force a person can express in a task. Early gains can be strongly neural and skill-specific; longer-term gains may also involve muscle growth and architectural change. Heavier loading generally produces larger gains in tested one-repetition-maximum strength than lighter loading, especially when the test resembles the training. [SRC-043][SRC-044]

### Muscular power

Power is force expressed quickly. It depends on force capacity, rate of force development, movement skill, and the load being moved. The 2026 ACSM evidence synthesis found that moderate loads moved with fast concentric intent and low-to-moderate volume tend to support power better than simply lifting every set slowly or to exhaustion. [SRC-043]

### Hypertrophy

Hypertrophy is increased muscle size. Many load ranges can produce it when the work is sufficiently challenging, while more weekly set volume tends to matter more than load alone. This does not mean every athlete needs to maximize muscle mass. [SRC-043][SRC-044]

### Local muscular endurance

Local endurance is the ability to repeat or sustain force within a particular task. High-repetition work can develop it, but a high-repetition lunge circuit is not equivalent to high-load strength training even if both feel hard.

## The prescription variables

### Load

Load is the resistance relative to current capacity. It can be described as a percentage of one-repetition maximum, a repetition range, velocity, or effort. Higher loads have the clearest advantage when maximal strength is the outcome. Lower loads can still improve strength and muscle size, especially in novices, but they usually require more repetitions and may create a different fatigue profile. [SRC-043][SRC-044]

### Sets and weekly volume

Volume can be counted as hard sets, repetitions, tonnage, or work. None is a complete biological dose. Multiple sets generally support more strength and hypertrophy than a single set, but returns diminish and costs rise. The number of useful sets depends on exercise, training status, proximity to failure, other sport load, and recovery. [SRC-043][SRC-044]

### Frequency

Frequency distributes volume and practice. More days are not automatically a stronger stimulus when weekly work is equated. A frequency is useful when it makes quality work repeatable, not because the calendar itself creates adaptation.

### Range of motion and exercise selection

A complete, controlled range of motion usually supports broad strength development, but anatomy, goal, symptoms, and sport specificity can justify a different range. Exercise selection should cover the intended joint actions and be stable enough to load and progress. Complexity is not adaptation.

### Proximity to failure

Momentary failure is the point at which another complete repetition cannot be performed with the intended technique. It is not required for strength or hypertrophy. Meta-analytic evidence does not show that taking every set to momentary failure is superior; failure also increases fatigue and can reduce the quality of later work. [SRC-043][SRC-054]

### Progression

Progressive overload means the training challenge develops as capacity develops. Progress can be more load, repetitions, sets, range, speed, control, or a harder variation. It does not require increasing all variables, or progressing every week.

## What general evidence supports

### Claim

Progressive resistance training improves strength, muscle size, power, muscular endurance, movement velocity, balance, and several physical-function outcomes in healthy adults compared with no training. [SRC-043]

**Evidence:** Strong\
**Population:** Healthy adults, with broad age and training-status representation across 137 reviews\
**Type:** Position stand and overview of systematic reviews\
**Confidence:** High for broad benefit; moderate for exact prescription\
**Important caveat:** General-adult adaptation is not proof that the same program improves running performance.

### Claim

Almost every studied combination of load, sets, and weekly frequency improves strength and hypertrophy versus no resistance training; heavier loading ranks higher for maximal strength, while multiple sets are more influential for hypertrophy. [SRC-044]

**Evidence:** Strong\
**Population:** Healthy adults; 178 studies in the strength network and 119 in the hypertrophy network\
**Type:** Systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis\
**Confidence:** High for broad direction\
**Important caveat:** A ranking across group means is not an individualized optimum, and many studies involve people who are not endurance athletes.

## Minimum dose and adherence

Low-volume high-load training and brief, frequent resistance “snacks” can improve strength or physical function in younger and older adults. The evidence is promising when time, access, confidence, or adherence is the limiting constraint. [SRC-053]

“Minimal dose” means the smallest dose that still advances the target outcome in the studied context. It does not mean:

- every brief bodyweight circuit produces the same adaptations as heavy resistance;
- the dose is sufficient forever;
- the lowest possible dose maximizes performance;
- general-population results automatically transfer to trained runners.

Maintenance is a different problem from development. In a general-adult trial, substantially reduced volume retained many strength adaptations after a 16-week development phase, while muscle-size retention was less robust in older adults. [SRC-062] This supports reducing cost after a gain has been built; it does not define one maintenance set or frequency for every runner.

## Concurrent endurance and strength training

Strength and endurance can improve in the same program. Interference is not an all-or-nothing molecular switch; it is an outcome shaped by athlete status, endurance mode and volume, lower-body fatigue, scheduling, nutrition, and the strength outcome being pursued. A 2024 meta-analysis found a small attenuation of lower-body strength gains in men but not women, with important data gaps in women and highly trained athletes. [SRC-050]

For an endurance athlete, the practical question is rarely “Can these coexist?” It is “Which adaptation has priority, and what strength dose can be absorbed without degrading the endurance work that matters more?”

## A stimulus-cost model

| Method | Primary stimulus | Common cost | What it is not |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-load compound resistance | Maximal force, neural skill, muscle/tendon loading | Neuromuscular fatigue, soreness, technical demand | A guarantee of running transfer |
| Moderate-load fast-intent work | Power and rate of force expression | Coordination demand, velocity loss with fatigue | The same as plyometrics |
| Higher-volume resistance | Hypertrophy and local work capacity | Soreness, time, energy demand | Automatically better for endurance athletes |
| Isometrics | Angle-specific force and sustained tension | Local fatigue; angle specificity | A universally proven tendon treatment |
| Bodyweight/band circuits | Adherence, movement practice, local endurance | Can become repetitive high-repetition fatigue | Equivalent to high-load strength |
| Minimal-dose training | Low logistical barrier and a maintainable stimulus | May underdose advanced outcomes | “Nothing is enough” |

## Best fit

- The target adaptation is explicit.
- The athlete can perform and progress the selected movements safely.
- The dose leaves enough recovery for higher-priority work.
- The program is simple enough to execute consistently and measure.
- A novice first develops exercise skill and repeatability before chasing maximal loading.

## Poor fit

- Selecting exercises because they look athletic rather than because they train the target.
- Maximizing strength, power, hypertrophy, and local endurance simultaneously.
- Taking every set to failure.
- Adding load, sets, complexity, and frequency at once.
- Treating soreness as evidence of productive overload.
- Using a general strength guideline as a runner-specific race prescription.

## What remains individual

- exercise selection and acceptable range of motion;
- starting load and volume;
- tolerance for eccentric soreness;
- the amount of hypertrophy that supports or conflicts with the sport goal;
- scheduling relative to endurance sessions;
- the dose needed for maintenance after a gain;
- symptom-specific modifications, which may require clinical assessment.

## Open questions

- What is the minimum maintenance dose for different strength and power outcomes in trained endurance athletes?
- Which prescription variables matter most after several years of concurrent training?
- How should sex, age, menstrual status, and menopause change strength programming rather than merely subgroup interpretation?
- Which field measures identify useful adaptation without turning training into constant testing?
