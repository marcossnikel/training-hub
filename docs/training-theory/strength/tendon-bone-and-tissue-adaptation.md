# Tendon, bone, and tissue adaptation

## Why these tissues need separate reasoning

Muscle, tendon, bone, cartilage, and the nervous system respond to different features of load and on different time courses. A runner can improve aerobic fitness before accumulating equivalent running-specific tissue exposure. Strength training can add useful mechanical loading, but “stronger tissues” is not one measurable adaptation.

This document addresses healthy-tissue adaptation and evidence boundaries. It is not a rehabilitation protocol and does not diagnose pain, tendinopathy, low bone density, or bone stress injury.

## Tendon adaptation

Tendons transmit muscle force and store/return energy. Relevant properties include stiffness, elastic modulus, cross-sectional area, and the behavior of the muscle-tendon unit. More stiffness is not universally better; the useful behavior depends on the task, tendon, muscle capacity, and individual.

### Claim

Chronic mechanical loading produces moderate increases in lower-limb tendon stiffness, large increases in modulus, and small increases in cross-sectional area on average. Resistance training and protocols producing higher localized tendon strain showed larger adaptations than lower-strain protocols. [SRC-051]

**Evidence:** Moderate\
**Population:** 763 healthy participants across 61 studies; Achilles and patellar tendons dominated\
**Type:** Systematic review and meta-analysis\
**Confidence:** Moderate for healthy-tendon property change\
**Important caveat:** The studies do not establish a runner-specific injury-prevention dose, and adaptation in an asymptomatic tendon is not treatment evidence for a painful tendon.

Practical implications are conditional:

- a tendon needs sufficient repeated tension to adapt;
- the same external exercise can create different local strain across athletes;
- higher strain may be a larger stimulus and a larger acute cost;
- properties adapt over repeated weeks, not from one “activation” session;
- pain response and clinical diagnosis cannot be inferred from stiffness change.

## Muscle and force capacity

Resistance training improves strength, power, muscle size, and physical function in healthy adults. These outcomes can support tissue loading indirectly because greater force capacity may make a submaximal task a smaller fraction of maximum. It can also increase the force an athlete is capable of applying; capacity and exposure therefore need to develop together. [SRC-043][SRC-044]

Soreness is a poor proxy for adaptation. It is more strongly influenced by novelty, eccentric work, range, and individual response than by whether the long-term dose is optimal.

## Bone adaptation

Bone responds to strain magnitude, rate, direction, distribution, recovery, hormonal context, and energy availability. Response is site-specific: a loading program can affect the spine, hip, or another region differently. Bone-mineral density is also not the complete description of bone strength.

### Claim

In premenopausal women, programs combining odd- or high-impact exercise with high-magnitude resistance training produced small improvements in lumbar-spine and femoral-neck bone-mineral density; high-impact-only protocols improved femoral-neck density in that review. [SRC-052]

**Evidence:** Moderate for a small site-specific group-average effect\
**Population:** Premenopausal women in controlled exercise trials\
**Type:** Systematic review and meta-analysis\
**Confidence:** Moderate for direction, limited for runner application\
**Important caveat:** The trials were methodologically diverse, some publication bias was possible, and the finding is not an individualized prescription for low BMD or bone stress injury.

Running already supplies impact, but repeated straight-line impact is not identical to high-magnitude resistance or multidirectional loading. More impact is also not automatically beneficial when energy availability, recovery, menstrual/endocrine function, or symptoms are compromised.

## Energy availability changes the loading context

The IOC RED-S consensus links problematic low energy availability with impaired bone health and other health/performance consequences in female and male athletes. No exercise selection can compensate for chronically inadequate energy availability. [SRC-025]

Persistent bone pain, recurrent bone stress injury, menstrual disturbance, loss of libido, disordered eating, or unexplained performance decline requires qualified assessment. A strength article is not a substitute for sports-medicine, nutrition, or endocrine care.

## Tissue adaptation versus injury prevention

These are different claims:

1. an exercise loads a tissue;
2. a tissue property changes;
3. the athlete tolerates more sport exposure;
4. pain improves;
5. injury incidence falls.

Evidence for an earlier step cannot prove a later one. Runner prevention reviews and a large randomized marathoner trial did not show an overall injury reduction from the tested exercise programs. [SRC-042][SRC-049]

This does not mean tissue loading is useless. It means injury prevention cannot be promised from force capacity, tendon stiffness, or bone-density mechanisms alone.

## Coach practice under the evidence boundary

### Coaching hypothesis: frequent hamstring isometrics

Roche describes a single-leg hamstring isometric hold that he personally uses for high-hamstring weakness or discomfort, with a two-leg warm-up and short alternating holds. He explicitly frames it through personal experience and athlete use. [SRC-059]

**Evidence:** Anecdotal coach practice for this exact routine\
**Coach practice:** Brief, repeatable hamstring isometric exposure\
**Potential benefit:** Low-friction local force practice\
**Potential cost:** An online exercise can aggravate the wrong diagnosis or dose\
**Best fit:** As a documented coaching example, or inside a clinician-approved plan—not as autonomous treatment advice

### Coaching hypothesis: strength and loading for bone health

Roche and Megan Roche describe squats, deadlifts, presses, and loaded daily activity inside a much broader bone-density article. The strength/loading idea is compatible with general evidence, but the exact exercise doses, weighted-vest protocol, supplement amounts, hormonal treatment, and medications are not validated here. [SRC-052][SRC-060]

**Evidence:** Moderate for exercise supporting bone health in some populations; limited for the exact SWAP package\
**Coach practice:** Resistance and additional weight-bearing exposure\
**Potential benefit:** Site-specific mechanical stimulus\
**Potential cost:** Excess loading, false reassurance, or delayed clinical care\
**Best fit:** General concept interpreted with medical and nutrition context

The repository does not reproduce or endorse the article's clinical, supplement, hormone, or medication specifics. Those require individualized medical management.

## A load-capacity model

| Tissue/outcome | Relevant loading features | Useful observation | What cannot be inferred |
|---|---|---|---|
| Muscle strength | Force, range, sets, effort, progression | Load/repetitions with stable technique | Injury immunity |
| Tendon properties | Local strain, duration, repetition, recovery | Force task and symptom response over time | Diagnosis or ideal stiffness |
| Bone | Strain magnitude/rate/direction, site, recovery, energy status | Long-term exposure and clinical measures when indicated | That more impact is always better |
| Running tolerance | Sport-specific impact and repetition | Stable symptoms and recovery across weeks | Safety from one pain-free session |
| Injury incidence | Capacity plus total exposure and many contextual factors | Prospective population outcome | From a surrogate alone |

## Best fit

- Healthy athletes building capacity gradually.
- Strength work that complements rather than duplicates the week's mechanical stress.
- Site- and goal-specific loading with stable recovery.
- Bone-health work paired with adequate energy availability and clinical care when indicated.

## Poor fit

- Treating pain from a generic article.
- Adding high-rate impact to suspected bone stress injury.
- Assuming tendon stiffness is always beneficial.
- Using weighted loading to compensate for underfueling.
- Promising injury prevention from a stronger squat.
- Progressing because a tissue is not sore immediately.

## Open questions

- Which healthy-tendon adaptations improve running performance or resilience, and which are neutral?
- What runner-specific loading combinations best support bone health across sex and life stage?
- How should strength, plyometrics, sprinting, hills, and downhill running be combined into one mechanical-load model?
- Which supervised programs reduce actual running injuries rather than only changing surrogate capacity?
- How do energy availability and endocrine status modify tissue response to the same external load?
