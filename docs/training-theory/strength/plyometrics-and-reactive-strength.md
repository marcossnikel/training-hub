# Plyometrics and reactive strength for runners

## Definition

Plyometric training uses rapid eccentric-to-concentric actions to train the stretch-shortening cycle. Hops, bounds, drop jumps, countermovement jumps, and some skipping drills can qualify. Reactive strength is the ability to change rapidly from braking to propulsion; it is not merely jump height or leg strength.

Plyometrics differ from:

- **heavy resistance**, which emphasizes high force against large external load;
- **power resistance**, which moves an external load quickly;
- **drills**, which may emphasize rhythm or coordination without a large overload;
- **strides and sprints**, which are running-specific high-force exposures;
- **high-repetition circuits**, which emphasize local endurance more than short-contact reactivity.

## Why runners may use them

Running repeatedly stores and returns elastic energy while muscles coordinate short ground contacts. Plyometrics may improve rate of force development, tendon-muscle behavior, reactive strength, coordination, and the ability to produce force rapidly. Better values in these intermediates do not guarantee better running economy or race performance.

## What the evidence shows

### Claim

Across 21 studies and 511 endurance runners, jump training improved several jump, reactive-strength, sprint, and rate-of-force-development measures and produced a moderate pooled effect on 2–5 km time-trial performance. It did not improve VO2max, vVO2max, or lactate-related measures. [SRC-047]

**Evidence:** Moderate for neuromuscular outcomes; mixed for endurance performance\
**Population:** Endurance runners across heterogeneous levels and interventions\
**Type:** Systematic review and meta-analysis\
**Confidence:** Moderate for direction, lower for exact magnitude\
**Important caveat:** Small studies, varied jump types and running tests, and incomplete reporting limit dose inference.

### Claim

In a 2024 running-economy synthesis, plyometric training produced a small benefit primarily at test speeds at or below 12 km/h. A separate 2024 performance synthesis did not find a statistically significant pooled performance effect from plyometrics alone. [SRC-013][SRC-045]

**Evidence:** Mixed\
**Population:** Middle- and long-distance runners\
**Type:** Systematic reviews and meta-analyses\
**Confidence:** Moderate that response depends on protocol and outcome\
**Important caveat:** These syntheses include overlapping small studies and do not establish a universal speed cutoff or optimal jump program.

## Interpreting disagreement

The evidence does not reduce to “plyometrics work” or “plyometrics do not work.” Different reviews pool different eligibility criteria, outcomes, test speeds, and intervention categories. Improvements in jump performance are more direct than improvements in running; economy and time-trial outcomes are downstream and noisier.

Heavy resistance currently has the clearer pooled signal for runner economy and performance. Plyometrics may still be attractive when reactive strength is the target, when the athlete already has a strength base, or as part of a combined program that can be recovered from. [SRC-045][SRC-046]

## Dose is more than contacts

A jump contact is not a standardized unit. Cost changes with:

- bilateral versus unilateral execution;
- vertical versus horizontal direction;
- jump height and landing speed;
- rigid versus forgiving surface;
- countermovement versus drop-jump entry;
- ground-contact time and intent;
- athlete mass, skill, fatigue, and footwear;
- whether the same week also adds sprints, hills, or downhill running.

Therefore, “100 contacts” can describe very different loads. Count contacts for tracking, but also classify intensity, direction, complexity, and novelty.

## A progression logic

1. Establish pain-free running and basic landing/control exposure.
2. Learn low-amplitude bilateral contacts with consistent rhythm.
3. Increase only one meaningful dimension: contacts, amplitude, direction, speed, or unilateral demand.
4. Preserve quality; stop a set when contact, alignment, or intent materially deteriorates.
5. Add high-intensity drop jumps, bounding, or unilateral work only when the prior level is repeatable.
6. Reassess the rest of the week: fast running and downhill work belong in the same mechanical-load picture.

This is a risk-management framework, not a validated universal ladder.

## Placement and fatigue

Novel plyometrics can create soreness and reduce neuromuscular output. Runner evidence on acute strength sessions shows that peak torque, soreness, and perceived effort can remain affected even when submaximal oxygen cost and heart rate look normal. [SRC-048]

Useful placement principles:

- quality contacts usually belong when coordination is still good;
- a small familiar dose can be paired with a quality day to consolidate stress;
- high-volume or novel jumps before a key running workout can degrade the target session;
- “easy day” refers to the whole day, not just the run pace;
- do not increase jumping, sprinting, and downhill exposure together without acknowledging the combined novelty.

## Tendon and bone are not automatic outcomes

Mechanical loading can increase lower-limb tendon stiffness and modulus, with higher local strain showing larger adaptations on average. The tendon literature is not specific to plyometrics or runners and does not establish that stiffer is always better, that pain has been treated, or that injury risk falls. [SRC-051]

Jumping can be osteogenic in some populations, but bone response is site-specific and slow. Evidence from premenopausal women supports some combinations of impact and high-magnitude resistance exercise; it does not validate an exact jump dose for a runner with low bone density or bone pain. [SRC-052]

## Best fit

- The target is reactive strength or rapid force expression.
- The athlete has stable running load and basic landing skill.
- A small jump dose can be progressed without displacing key running.
- The sport demands short contacts, hills, or changes in force direction.
- The athlete and coach can monitor total high-rate mechanical exposure.

## Poor fit

- Current bone, tendon, or joint pain without assessment.
- A novice runner adding jumps because the metabolic cost feels low.
- Using maximal drop height as a marker of toughness.
- Chasing contact count after output and technique decline.
- Treating soreness or stiffness as proof of adaptation.
- Assuming a jump improvement guarantees marathon improvement.

## Open questions

- Which jump characteristics best transfer to running at different speeds and gradients?
- What is the minimum effective and maintenance dose for trained runners?
- How should total contacts be weighted by height, direction, and unilateral demand?
- Which athletes benefit more from plyometrics than from additional heavy resistance?
- Does plyometric work improve economy durability late in prolonged running?
